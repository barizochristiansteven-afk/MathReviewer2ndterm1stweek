A short note before we begin

Dear student,

Welcome to Week 1 of General Mathematics. This is the week where you meet one of the most quietly useful ideas in all of mathematics — the piecewise function. It has a long name, but the idea behind it is something you have already lived your whole life.

Think about the last time you rode a jeepney. You did not pay a random amount. You paid a flat fare for the first few kilometres, and then a small extra charge for each kilometre beyond that. The driver did not do complicated math in his head. He simply knew two rules: one for short rides, one for longer rides. Whichever rule matched your trip, that was the price.

That is a piecewise function. It is one function, but it carries more than one rule. The rule you use depends on the number you were given. Nothing more, nothing less.

You will see the same idea everywhere once you start looking. Your phone plan charges one rate for the first five gigabytes, and a higher rate after that. Your electric bill climbs slowly at first, then faster once you cross a certain number of kilowatt-hours. A parking garage charges one rate for the first two hours, then a new rate after. Even income tax is built from brackets — a chain of piecewise rules stacked on top of each other.

Take your time. Read the examples slowly. Drag the slider. Test yourself with the fifty questions and read the explanation after each one, whether you got it right or wrong. By the time you finish, piecewise functions will not feel like a new topic. They will feel like a lens — a way of seeing the pricing rules that were always quietly running in the background of your everyday life.

What a piecewise function actually is

A piecewise function is one function that behaves differently depending on the input. Instead of a single rule that applies everywhere, it carries a short list of rules, and each rule is responsible for a specific range of values.

Everyday comparison A jeepney driver follows two rules. Ride 4 km or less, and you pay a flat fare. Ride farther, and the extra distance is added on top of that same flat fare. Two rules, one total price — that is piecewise.

What makes it useful is that the real world rarely charges a single rate for everything. Water, electricity, parking, overtime, shipping, taxes: all of them change their rate once you cross a threshold.

The method that always works

Every problem in this reviewer follows the same three moves.

  1. Read the value you were given.
  2. Check the conditions one by one and find the one that is true.
  3. Apply only that rule and compute carefully.
Common mistake Do not blend two rules together, and do not fall back to the rule that looks easiest. The condition decides everything. If the number does not satisfy a rule's condition, that rule is off the table.

A tiny example to warm up

Suppose a function says:

p(x) = 5if x < 10
p(x) = 5 + 2(x − 10)if x ≥ 10

p(3): 3 is less than 10, so use the first rule. p(3) = 5.

p(10): The symbol is ≥, which includes 10. p(10) = 5 + 2(0) = 5.

p(13): p(13) = 5 + 2(3) = 11.

Notice how p(10) gives the same answer under both rules. That is on purpose — the two pieces were designed to meet at the boundary.

Jeepney fare: worked examples

Ride of 3 km. 3 ≤ 4, so rule 1. f(3) = ₱12.00.

Ride of 4 km. Rule 1, since ≤ includes 4. f(4) = ₱12.00.

Ride of 5 km. f(5) = 12 + 1.5(1) = ₱13.50.

Ride of 7 km. f(7) = 12 + 1.5(3) = 12 + 4.50 = ₱16.50.

Ride of 10 km. f(10) = 12 + 1.5(6) = 12 + 9.00 = ₱21.00.

Ride of 12 km. f(12) = 12 + 1.5(8) = 12 + 12.00 = ₱24.00.

Ride of 4.5 km. f(4.5) = 12 + 1.5(0.5) = 12 + 0.75 = ₱12.75. Only half a kilometre is extra.

Working backwards

You paid ₱18.00. How far did you ride?

₱18 is more than ₱12, so the ride must be in rule 2.
12 + 1.5(x − 4) = 18
1.5(x − 4) = 6
x − 4 = 4
x = 8 km.

Check: 12 + 1.5(8 − 4) = 12 + 6 = ₱18. Correct.

The trap to avoid Do not write 1.5x instead of 1.5(x − 4). The first 4 km are already covered by the ₱12 base. If you charge 1.5 on all 10 km of a 10 km ride, you are charging twice for the first 4 km.

The warm-up function, fully worked

This is the function from your notes. Let us work through it slowly.

{
g(x) = 3x + 1if x < 0
g(x) = x² + 3if x ≥ 0

Negative inputs

g(−1) = 3(−1) + 1 = −2.

g(−3) = 3(−3) + 1 = −8.

g(−5) = 3(−5) + 1 = −14.

g(−0.5) = 3(−0.5) + 1 = −0.5.

Zero and positive inputs

g(0) = 0² + 3 = 3 (the ≥ includes 0).

g(1) = 1 + 3 = 4.

g(3) = 9 + 3 = 12.

g(5) = 25 + 3 = 28.

g(10) = 100 + 3 = 103.

g(0.5) = 0.25 + 3 = 3.25.

Spotting the boundary

At x = 0, rule 1 would give 3(0) + 1 = 1, but rule 1 does not include 0. Rule 2 gives 0 + 3 = 3. So g(0) = 3, and the graph jumps from a value near 1 (just below 0) up to 3 (at 0). This is a common feature of piecewise functions — the boundary is where the action happens.

Mobile data plan

A telecom company charges ₱300 per month for a plan that includes 5 GB of data. Any data beyond 5 GB costs ₱50 per gigabyte.

{
D(g) = 300if 0 ≤ g ≤ 5
D(g) = 300 + 50(g − 5)if g > 5

3 GB used. D(3) = ₱300. Inside the plan, no extra charge.

5 GB used. The ≤ includes 5. D(5) = ₱300.

6 GB used. D(6) = 300 + 50(1) = ₱350.

8 GB used. D(8) = 300 + 50(3) = ₱450.

10 GB used. D(10) = 300 + 50(5) = ₱550.

12 GB used. D(12) = 300 + 50(7) = ₱650.

15 GB used. D(15) = 300 + 50(10) = ₱800.

Notice that D(g) never goes below ₱300, no matter how little data you use. That is what it means to be a flat monthly plan.

Bulk pencil purchase

A bookstore sells pencils at ₱10 each. If a customer buys 10 or more, every pencil drops to ₱8 each.

{
C(p) = 10pif 0 < p < 10
C(p) = 8pif p ≥ 10

3 pencils. C(3) = ₱30.

5 pencils. C(5) = ₱50.

8 pencils. C(8) = ₱80.

9 pencils. C(9) = ₱90.

10 pencils. The ≥ includes 10. C(10) = 8 × 10 = ₱80.

12 pencils. C(12) = ₱96.

15 pencils. C(15) = ₱120.

20 pencils. C(20) = ₱160.

The surprising result Nine pencils cost ₱90. Ten pencils cost ₱80. Buying one more pencil saves you ₱10. This is a real thing that happens with bulk pricing.

Hourly wage with overtime

An employee earns ₱80 per hour for up to 40 hours a week. Any time beyond 40 hours is paid at 1.5 times the regular rate, which is ₱120 per hour.

{
W(h) = 80hif 0 ≤ h ≤ 40
W(h) = 3200 + 120(h − 40)if h > 40

Where did the 3200 come from? It is the pay for the first 40 hours: 80 × 40 = ₱3,200. That flat amount is baked into the second rule.

20 hours. W(20) = 80 × 20 = ₱1,600.

35 hours. W(35) = ₱2,800.

40 hours. W(40) = ₱3,200.

41 hours. W(41) = 3200 + 120(1) = ₱3,320.

45 hours. W(45) = 3200 + 120(5) = ₱3,800.

50 hours. W(50) = 3200 + 120(10) = ₱4,400.

Working backwards

A worker earned ₱4,400. How many hours did they work?

3200 + 120(h − 40) = 4400
120(h − 40) = 1200
h − 40 = 10
h = 50 hours.

Trap to avoid Do not compute 80 × 41 = ₱3,280 for a 41-hour week. The 41st hour is overtime and must be paid at ₱120.

Parking rates and the ceiling function

A parking garage charges ₱40 for the first 2 hours, then ₱20 for each extra hour or fraction of an hour. That phrase “or fraction” is what brings the ceiling function in.

{
P(t) = 40if 0 < t ≤ 2
P(t) = 40 + 20⌈t − 2⌉if t > 2

2 hours. P(2) = ₱40.

2.5 hours. ⌈0.5⌉ = 1. P(2.5) = ₱60.

3 hours. P(3) = ₱60.

3.5 hours. ⌈1.5⌉ = 2. P(3.5) = ₱80.

4.5 hours. ⌈2.5⌉ = 3. P(4.5) = ₱100.

6 hours. ⌈4⌉ = 4. P(6) = ₱120.

8 hours. ⌈6⌉ = 6. P(8) = ₱160.

Ceiling and floor, side by side

⌈3.5⌉ = 4  ·  ⌈2.1⌉ = 3  ·  ⌈2.01⌉ = 3  ·  ⌈7⌉ = 7

⌊3.9⌋ = 3  ·  ⌊4.99⌋ = 4  ·  ⌊6⌋ = 6

Why parking uses ceiling If you park for two hours and ten minutes, you have entered a third hour, even if only briefly. The garage rounds that sliver up to a full hour. The ceiling function always rounds up.

The water bill, worked in full

This one has four tiers. Each tier has a flat amount plus a per-cubic-metre charge for anything beyond the tier's starting point.

{
W(x) = 200if 0 < x ≤ 10
W(x) = 200 + 30(x − 10)if 10 < x ≤ 20
W(x) = 500 + 40(x − 20)if 20 < x ≤ 30
W(x) = 900 + 35(x − 30)if x > 30

5 m³. W(5) = ₱200.

10 m³. W(10) = ₱200.

12 m³. W(12) = 200 + 30(2) = ₱260.

15 m³. W(15) = ₱350.

20 m³. W(20) = 200 + 30(10) = ₱500.

24 m³. W(24) = 500 + 40(4) = ₱660.

30 m³. W(30) = 500 + 40(10) = ₱900.

34 m³. W(34) = 900 + 35(4) = ₱1,040.

The challenge: working backwards

A household received a bill of ₱1,040. How much water did they use?

900 + 35(x − 30) = 1040
35(x − 30) = 140
x − 30 = 4
x = 34 m³.

The environmental tax

Tax applies only to consumption strictly above 30 m³. For 34 m³:
Amount above: 4 m³. Charge on that portion: 35 × 4 = ₱140.
Tax at 15%: 0.15 × 140 = ₱21.
Total payable: 1040 + 21 = ₱1,061.

Trap to avoid Do not take 15% of the whole ₱1,040 bill. That gives ₱156, which is far too high. The rule restricts the tax to consumption inside the highest bracket only.
