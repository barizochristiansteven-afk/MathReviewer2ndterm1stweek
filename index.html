Week 1

A short note before we begin

Dear student,

Welcome to Week 1 of General Mathematics. This week you will meet the piecewise function. The name sounds long, but the idea behind it is something you already know from real life.

Imagine you ride a jeepney. If you only travel a short distance, you pay a flat minimum fare. But if you travel farther, the driver adds a little more for every extra kilometre. Two rules. One price.

A piecewise function works the same way. It is one function that carries more than one rule. You look at the number you were given, you check which rule matches, and you use that rule only.

Once you understand this, you will start noticing it everywhere: phone plans, water bills, parking fees, overtime pay, and even income tax.

Let us begin.
— Your Week 1 reviewer

What you will learn this week

By the end of Week 1, you will be able to do three things:

  1. Write a piecewise function from a real-life story — like a jeepney fare, a print shop price, or a parking fee.
  2. Solve piecewise problems by picking the right rule and doing the math carefully.
  3. Explain why one rule is not enough for the real world. Some prices stay flat, then change after a certain point.

What is a piecewise function?

Think of a piecewise function as a rule book with more than one rule. When someone gives you a number, you flip open the rule book, find the rule that matches that number, and follow only that one.

Real-life picture A jeepney driver follows two rules.
Rule 1: If you ride 5 km or less, pay ₱13.
Rule 2: If you ride more than 5 km, pay ₱13 plus ₱1 for every extra kilometre.

Two rules. One fare. That is what makes it piecewise.

Why does the real world use this? Because most prices do not stay the same forever. Water, electricity, parking, and phone plans all change their rate once you go past a certain amount.

The three steps that always work

Every piecewise problem follows the same three simple steps.

  1. Read the number they gave you. That is your input.
  2. Check each rule one by one. Which "if" statement is true for your number?
  3. Use only that rule. Do the math carefully. Do not mix two rules together.
Watch out! Do not use the rule that looks easiest. Do not use two rules at once. The "if" part decides everything.

A small warm-up

Imagine a function that says:

p(x) = 5if x < 10
p(x) = 5 + 2(x − 10)if x ≥ 10

p(3): 3 is smaller than 10, so use rule 1. p(3) = 5.

p(10): The symbol ≥ includes 10, so use rule 2. p(10) = 5 + 2(0) = 5.

p(13): 13 is 10 or more, so use rule 2. p(13) = 5 + 2(3) = 11.

Notice: p(10) gives the same answer under both rules. That is on purpose. The two pieces were designed to meet at the boundary.

Jeepney fare: worked examples

Let us walk through seven different rides, one by one.

Ride of 3 km. 3 is 5 or less, so rule 1 applies. f(3) = ₱13.00.

Ride of 5 km. The symbol ≤ includes 5, so rule 1 still applies. f(5) = ₱13.00.

Ride of 6 km. 6 is more than 5, so rule 2 applies. f(6) = 6 + 8 = ₱14.00.

Ride of 7 km. f(7) = 7 + 8 = ₱15.00.

Ride of 10 km. f(10) = 10 + 8 = ₱18.00.

Ride of 12 km. f(12) = 12 + 8 = ₱20.00.

Working backwards

What if someone paid ₱20? How far did they ride?

₱20 is more than ₱13, so the ride must be in rule 2.
x + 8 = 20
x = 20 − 8
x = 12 km.

Check: f(12) = 12 + 8 = ₱20. Correct.

The warm-up function, slowly worked out

Here is a function with two rules:

{
f(x) = 2x + 1if x < 0
f(x) = x² + 3if x ≥ 0

When the number is negative

f(−1): −1 is less than 0, so use 2x + 1. f(−1) = 2(−1) + 1 = −2 + 1 = −1.

f(−3): f(−3) = 2(−3) + 1 = −6 + 1 = −5.

f(−5): f(−5) = 2(−5) + 1 = −10 + 1 = −9.

When the number is zero or positive

f(0): The ≥ sign includes 0, so use x² + 3. f(0) = 0 + 3 = 3.

f(2): f(2) = 2² + 3 = 4 + 3 = 7.

f(3): f(3) = 9 + 3 = 12.

f(5): f(5) = 25 + 3 = 28.

A nice surprise When x is small and negative, the answer is a small negative number. When x is 0 or bigger, the answer jumps up. That jump is normal — the two rules do not have to meet.

Print shop bulk buy

A print shop charges ₱5 for every page, but only up to 50 pages. Once you go past 50 pages, the first 50 pages cost a flat ₱250 and each extra page costs only ₱3.

{
C(p) = 5pif 0 < p ≤ 50
C(p) = 250 + 3(p − 50)if p > 50

25 pages: 25 is 50 or less, so use rule 1. C(25) = 5 × 25 = ₱125.

50 pages: The ≤ includes 50, so rule 1 still applies. C(50) = 5 × 50 = ₱250.

60 pages: 60 is more than 50, so use rule 2. C(60) = 250 + 3(10) = 250 + 30 = ₱280.

100 pages: C(100) = 250 + 3(50) = 250 + 150 = ₱400.

Why the rule changes The shop wants to reward you for printing a lot. That is why the price per page drops from ₱5 to ₱3 after 50 pages.

Internet plan at home

A home internet plan costs ₱999 every month. It includes up to 100 GB of data. If you go past 100 GB, every extra GB costs ₱10.

{
C(g) = 999if 0 ≤ g ≤ 100
C(g) = 999 + 10(g − 100)if g > 100

75 GB used: 75 is 100 or less, so rule 1 applies. C(75) = ₱999.

100 GB used: The ≤ includes 100, so rule 1 still applies. C(100) = ₱999.

130 GB used: 130 is more than 100, so use rule 2. C(130) = 999 + 10(30) = ₱1,299.

150 GB used: C(150) = 999 + 10(50) = ₱1,499.

A common trap Do not take 15% of the whole bill. That would give the wrong answer. Only the portion above 100 GB is charged the extra ₱10.

Mall parking fee

The mall charges ₱40 for the first 2 hours. After that, every extra hour or part of an hour costs ₱15.

The words "or part of an hour" mean we use the ceiling function.

{
P(h) = 40if 0 < h ≤ 2
P(h) = 40 + 15⌈h − 2⌉if h > 2

1.5 hours: 1.5 is 2 or less, so rule 1. P(1.5) = ₱40.

2 hours: The ≤ includes 2, so rule 1. P(2) = ₱40.

3.5 hours: Use rule 2. ⌈3.5 − 2⌉ = ⌈1.5⌉ = 2. P(3.5) = 40 + 15(2) = ₱70.

5 hours: ⌈5 − 2⌉ = ⌈3⌉ = 3. P(5) = 40 + 15(3) = ₱85.

Ceiling and floor, side by side

⌈3.5⌉ = 4  ·  ⌈2.1⌉ = 3  ·  ⌈7⌉ = 7

⌊3.9⌋ = 3  ·  ⌊4.99⌋ = 4  ·  ⌊6⌋ = 6

Why parking uses ceiling If you park for 2 hours and 10 minutes, you have stepped into a third hour. The garage rounds that small extra bit UP to a full hour. That is what the ceiling function does.

The water bill, worked in full

This one has four tiers. Each tier has a flat base plus a charge for every extra cubic metre.

{
W(x) = 200if 0 < x ≤ 10
W(x) = 200 + 30(x − 10)if 10 < x ≤ 20
W(x) = 500 + 40(x − 20)if 20 < x ≤ 30
W(x) = 900 + 35(x − 30)if x > 30

5 m³: 5 is 10 or less, so rule 1. W(5) = ₱200.

15 m³: Rule 2. W(15) = 200 + 30(5) = ₱350.

24 m³: Rule 3. W(24) = 500 + 40(4) = ₱660.

30 m³: Rule 3 (the ≤ includes 30). W(30) = 500 + 40(10) = ₱900.

34 m³: Rule 4. W(34) = 900 + 35(4) = ₱1,040.

Environmental tax

A 15% tax is charged only on the portion above 30 m³. For 34 m³:
Portion above 30: 4 m³.
Charge for that portion: 35 × 4 = ₱140.
Tax: 0.15 × 140 = ₱21.
Total payable: 1,040 + 21 = ₱1,061.

What you should remember from Week 1

Three big ideas to carry with you:

  1. A piecewise function is one function with more than one rule. Each rule covers a certain range of input values.
  2. To use it, follow three steps: read the number, check the rules, use the one that matches. Never mix two rules.
  3. Real-world prices change at thresholds — after 5 km, after 50 pages, after 100 GB, after 2 hours, and after 10 cubic metres. Piecewise functions describe those changes exactly.

Week 2

From rules to reasoning

Dear student,

Last week you learned how a single function can carry more than one rule. This week we move into statistics — the branch of math that helps us make sense of data.

Data is everywhere. The barangay health center collects it when they run a check-up. Your school collects it when they compare section scores. A sari-sari store owner collects it when they track daily earnings.

The question is not whether we have data. The question is: how do we describe it honestly?

This week you will learn three things. First, how to tell qualitative data from quantitative data. Second, how to sort data into the four levels of measurement. Third, how to pick the right measure of centre and spread for any situation.

Let us begin.
— Your Week 2 reviewer

What you will learn this week

By the end of Week 2, you will be able to do three things:

  1. Tell data types apart — decide whether a piece of data is qualitative or quantitative, and whether it is discrete or continuous.
  2. Sort data into four levels — nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio.
  3. Choose the right summary — mean, median, or mode for the centre, and range or standard deviation for the spread.
Why this matters When you describe data honestly, you help people make better decisions — a fairer loan program, a better nutrition plan, a smarter school policy.

Two families of data

Think of data as living in one of two big families. Knowing which family it belongs to tells you what you can and cannot do with it.

Family 1: Qualitative (or categorical) These are words, not numbers. They describe what kind something is. You cannot add them or average them.
Examples: blood type, favourite colour, gender, strand, barangay name.
Family 2: Quantitative (or numerical) These are numbers. They describe how many or how much. You can add, subtract, and average them.
Examples: age, height, monthly income, number of siblings.

A quick test: try adding two values together. If the answer makes sense, the data is quantitative. If the answer is nonsense, it is qualitative. (Adding "blue" and "red" gives nothing useful. Adding 3 siblings and 4 siblings gives 7 siblings.)

Discrete vs continuous numbers

Inside the quantitative family, numbers come in two flavours.

Discrete numbers You count these. They come in whole pieces. You cannot have half of one.
Examples: number of siblings (you can have 2, 3, or 4 — never 3.7), number of pets, number of books, number of school clubs.
Continuous numbers You measure these. They can take any value within a range, even tiny fractions.
Examples: height (158.4 cm or 158.42 cm), temperature (31.2°C), distance (3.2 km), study time (3.5 hours).

A quick test: between any two values, can you always find another value? If yes, the data is continuous. There are always more temperatures between 30°C and 31°C. But there is no "number of siblings" between 3 and 4.

The NOIR framework

Data is also grouped by how carefully it is measured. There are four levels. They spell the word NOIR.

  1. N — Nominal. Just names or labels. No order.
    Example: blood type A, B, AB, O.
  2. O — Ordinal. Labels with a natural order.
    Example: class rank 1st, 2nd, 3rd.
  3. I — Interval. Numbers with equal spacing, but no true zero.
    Example: temperature in Celsius.
  4. R — Ratio. Numbers with equal spacing and a true zero.
    Example: height, monthly income.

The higher the level, the more math you can do. Only ratio data lets you say "twice as much." Only interval and ratio let you use the mean. Ordinal allows the median. Nominal only allows the mode.

Nominal data (N in NOIR)

Nominal data is like a name tag. It tells you which group something belongs to. But it does not tell you which group is better or bigger.

Everyday examples Blood type (A, B, AB, O), favourite colour, gender, strand (STEM, HUMSS, ABM), barangay name, zip code.

What you can do with it: count how many fall in each group, and identify the most common one.

What you cannot do: put the groups in order, average them, or say one is "higher" than another. You cannot say the average blood type is AB. That sentence has no meaning.

Only measure of centre that works: the mode.

Ordinal data (O in NOIR)

Ordinal data is like a podium. There is a 1st place, a 2nd place, and a 3rd place. You know the order. But you cannot say the gap between 1st and 2nd is the same as the gap between 2nd and 3rd.

Everyday examples Class rank (1st, 2nd, 3rd), satisfaction (Very Satisfied, Satisfied, Neutral, Dissatisfied), difficulty (Easy, Medium, Hard), priority (High, Medium, Low), service rating (Excellent, Good, Fair, Poor).

What you can do: put them in order, and find the middle one.

What you cannot do: assume that the gaps between categories are equal. The difference between "Very Satisfied" and "Satisfied" is not necessarily the same as between "Neutral" and "Dissatisfied."

Best measure of centre: the median.

Interval data (I in NOIR)

Interval data is like a thermometer. Every degree is the same size. But there is no true zero — 0°C is just the freezing point of water. It does not mean "no temperature at all."

Everyday examples Temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit, calendar years, IQ scores.

What you can do: add and subtract values, and take the mean.

What you cannot do: take ratios. You cannot say 40°C is "twice as hot" as 20°C because 0°C does not mean "no heat."

Best measure of centre: the mean, unless there are extreme outliers. In that case, use the median.

Ratio data (R in NOIR)

Ratio data is like a measuring tape. It has equal spacing AND a true zero. Zero means none. That is what makes ratios meaningful.

Everyday examples Height, weight, monthly income, age, number of siblings, distance from school.

What you can do: everything. Add, subtract, average, and take ratios. You can say "₱40,000 is twice ₱20,000" because ₱0 means no income.

Best measure of centre: the mean, unless there are extreme outliers. Then use the median.

Variability: range for a quick look, standard deviation for the full picture.

Centre: mean, median, and mode

The centre of a data set is a single number that tries to describe the "typical" value. There are three ways to find it.

Mean = (sum of values) ÷ how manyaverage
Median = middle value when sortednot fooled by outliers
Mode = value that appears most oftenworks for categories

Let us try all three with the same data set: 2, 4, 4, 7, 8.

Mean: (2 + 4 + 4 + 7 + 8) ÷ 5 = 25 ÷ 5 = 5.

Median: The data is already in order. The middle one is 4. Median = 4.

Mode: 4 appears twice. All others appear once. Mode = 4.

When to use which Use the mean when there are no extreme values. Use the median when there is a very large or very small outlier. Use the mode when you are dealing with categories.

Spread: range, variance, and standard deviation

Knowing the centre is not enough. Two classes can have the same average score but feel very different. We need a number for how spread out the data is.

Range = max − minquick, uses only two values
Variance = average of squared gaps from the meandetailed
Std Dev = √variancesame units as data

Let us use the same data: 2, 4, 4, 7, 8.

Range: 8 − 2 = 6.

Mean: 5 (from the last slide).

Gaps from the mean: (2−5)²=9, (4−5)²=1, (4−5)²=1, (7−5)²=4, (8−5)²=9. Sum = 24.

Variance: 24 ÷ (5−1) = 24 ÷ 4 = 6.

Standard deviation: √6 ≈ 2.45.

Which one to use Range is a quick glance. Standard deviation gives the full story because it uses every number, not just the extremes.

Worked example: sari-sari store earnings

Ten sari-sari stores in Barangay Buenavista report their monthly earnings. Here they are, in order:

8,000 · 9,000 · 10,000 · 11,000 · 12,000 · 12,000 · 13,000 · 14,000 · 15,000 · 46,000

Let us compute each one

Mean: sum = 150,000 ÷ 10 = ₱15,000.

Median: middle two are 12,000 and 12,000. Average = ₱12,000.

Mode: 12,000 appears twice. Mode = ₱12,000.

Range: 46,000 − 8,000 = ₱38,000.

Standard deviation: about ₱11,447.

What does this tell us?

The mean is ₱15,000. The median is ₱12,000. That is a big difference. Why? Because one store earns ₱46,000. That one outlier pulls the mean up, making the average look higher than the typical store actually earns.

The median of ₱12,000 is more honest. It tells us what a "regular" sari-sari store earns.

The huge standard deviation of ₱11,447 tells us the earnings are very spread out. The stores do not have the same earning power. A loan program that assumes they do would be unfair to the smaller stores.

Choosing the right summary

Match the measure to the type of data you have.

NominalMode only
OrdinalMedian (mode as backup)
IntervalMean, or median if outliers
RatioMean, or median if outliers

For spread, use the range as a quick estimate, and standard deviation when you want the full picture.

The outlier rule If one value is very different from all the others, the mean gets pulled toward it. Use the median instead, and mention the outlier in your write-up.

What you should remember from Week 2

Three big ideas to carry with you:

  1. Data comes in two families: qualitative (words, categories) and quantitative (numbers). Inside the number family, values can be discrete (counted) or continuous (measured).
  2. The four levels of measurement are nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio. Each level allows different kinds of math.
  3. Use the mean for typical values when there are no outliers. Use the median when there is an outlier. Use the mode for categories. Use the standard deviation to describe spread.
Final thought Good statistics is honest statistics. When you report the right measure for the right data, you help people make better choices.
